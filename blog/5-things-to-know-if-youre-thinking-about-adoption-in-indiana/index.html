5 Things to Know If You're Thinking About Adoption in Indiana

Posted 7/20/2026 by Christopher L. Doran

Adopting a child is a big step. It is an exciting time, but it can also feel a bit scary. You might have a lot of questions about how the law works and what you need to do first.

As a small town lawyer here in North Vernon, I wear many hats. One of my favorite parts of my job is helping families grow through adoption. I believe in listening to what you have to say and giving you clear options. Whether you live in Vernon, Butlerville, Scipio, or Hayden, I am here to help you navigate this journey.

Indiana passed some new laws in 2026 (like SEA 171 and HB 1389) that change how adoption works. Here are five important things you should know if you are thinking about adoption this year.

1. The "12-Month Rule" Gives Families More Stability

One of the biggest changes in 2026 is for foster parents who want to adopt. In the past, things could feel uncertain if a child's placement changed suddenly. Now, the law helps keep things stable.

If a child has lived with you for at least 12 months, the law now assumes you have a "significant relationship" with that child. This is a big deal. If you file a petition to adopt, the court generally cannot move the child to a different home until they make a decision on your adoption case.

This change helps protect children from being moved around too much. It also gives families in Jennings County peace of mind while they wait for the court to finish the paperwork.

2. You Can Now Use Video for Adoption Paperwork

Giving consent is a major part of the adoption process. "Consent" is when a biological parent or a legal guardian agrees to the adoption.

In the past, this usually had to happen in person in front of a judge or a notary. But starting in 2026, Indiana made this much easier. You can now execute adoption consent via video conferencing.

This means if a parent lives far away or cannot travel to the courthouse in Vernon, they can still sign the papers while talking to an attorney over a video call. It makes the process faster and more convenient for everyone involved. I handle these types of family law matters often and can help you set up these digital meetings correctly.

3. The Home Study Is Your First Big Step

Before you can bring a child home, you must go through a "home study." This might sound intimidating, but it is just a way for the state to make sure a child will be safe and happy in your home.

During a home study, a social worker or agency will:

Visit your house to see where the child will sleep and play.

Check your background and finances.

Ask for references from people who know you well.

Talk to you about why you want to adopt.

Think of it as a chance to show how much love you have to give. It is a necessary part of almost every adoption, including private adoptions and foster-to-adopt cases. If you have questions about how to prepare, feel free to contact me to talk about the local requirements in Jennings County. You can also read more about whether stepparents and grandparents can skip the home study.

4. Open Adoption Can Start Sooner

Many families today choose "open adoption." This means the child might still have some contact with their biological family after the adoption is final. This is called "post-adoption contact."

The 2026 laws made a change here, too. Previously, a child had to be at least two years old before the court could approve these contact privileges. Now, the court can grant these privileges once the child is just 12 months old.

This allows families to create a plan that works for the child's best interests much earlier. Every family is different, and I can help you write an agreement that protects your rights while keeping the child's needs first.

5. Your Faith and Beliefs Are Protected

A new law (HB 1389) protects families from discrimination based on their religious beliefs. The government cannot stop you from adopting or fostering a child just because of your sincerely held religious views.

In fact, the law now says the state can consider your faith as a positive thing. If you and the child (or the child's birth parents) share the same religion, the court can use that information to help make a good match. This is great news for many families in our rural communities who want their faith to be a central part of their child's upbringing.
